Work Authorization

Work Authorization

The visa and status categories that determine whether — and how — a candidate can legally work in the US.

Simple explanation: A US Citizen and a Green Card holder (lawful permanent resident) can work for any US employer without sponsorship. An EAD (Employment Authorization Document) is a card that proves someone — like an asylee, refugee, or certain visa holder — is authorized to work, often independent of a specific employer.

Why it matters: These are generally the easiest candidates to place, because there's no employer sponsorship or visa transfer required.

Common mistakes: Assuming "EAD" means the same thing for every candidate — an EAD can come from many different underlying statuses, each with its own renewal timeline.

Simple explanation: H1B is a specialty-occupation visa tied to one sponsoring employer. H4 EAD is a work permit for the spouse of an H1B holder. L1 is for employees transferring within the same multinational company; L2 is for the spouse of an L1 holder.

Why it matters: H1B and L1 candidates usually can't switch employers overnight — moving them requires a formal visa transfer process, which affects how quickly they can start a new role.

Common mistakes: Promising a candidate a fast start date without first checking whether their visa type requires a transfer process.

Simple explanation: OPT (Optional Practical Training) and CPT (Curricular Practical Training) are temporary work authorizations for F1 international students, usually tied to their field of study. TN is a visa specifically for Canadian and Mexican citizens under the USMCA trade agreement.

Why it matters: OPT has a time limit (typically 12 months, longer for STEM fields) after which the candidate needs a new status like H1B — this affects long-term planning for a placement.

Common mistakes: Not asking how much time is left on a candidate's OPT before submitting them for a long-term role.

Simple explanation: Refugees and asylees are generally authorized to work in the US without employer sponsorship. "Sponsorship" means an employer formally supports a candidate's visa or Green Card process. A "visa transfer" moves an existing visa, like H1B, from one employer to another.

Why it matters: Whether a client is willing to sponsor, or accept a visa transfer, directly determines which candidates you can even submit for a given job order.

Common mistakes: Submitting a candidate who needs sponsorship to a job order explicitly marked "no sponsorship" — it wastes everyone's time and can hurt the agency's credibility with the client.

Interview Questions

Summary

Work authorization determines two things: whether a candidate can work at all, and whether your specific client is willing to take them on given the sponsorship or transfer required. Always confirm status and timeline early — before you invest time pitching a role the candidate legally can't start.

This page is educational, not immigration or legal advice. Always verify a candidate's current status and documents directly.
