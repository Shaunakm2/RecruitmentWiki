EEOC

What the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission covers, and the questions recruiters must never ask candidates.

Simple explanation: The EEOC (Equal Employment Opportunity Commission) enforces federal laws that make it illegal to discriminate against a candidate or employee based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, age (40 and older), disability, or genetic information.

Why it matters: These protections apply throughout the entire recruiting process — from job ads to screening questions to who gets submitted — not just after someone is hired.

Common mistakes: Assuming EEOC rules only apply to the client's HR team, not to a third-party recruiter. Recruiters can be held to the same standard.

Simple explanation: Recruiters should avoid directly asking about a candidate's age, religion, marital or family status, national origin, disability, or plans to have children — these all relate to protected characteristics.

Why it matters: Even an innocent, curious question ("Do you have kids?") can be read as screening for a protected characteristic, exposing the agency and client to legal risk.

Common mistakes: Asking these questions casually as small talk on a call, thinking it's harmless rapport-building rather than a compliance issue.

Simple explanation: Job ads shouldn't specify preferences like age (even indirectly, e.g. "recent college grad") or gender-coded language, and screening criteria should be based on job-related skills and qualifications, not personal characteristics.

Why it matters: Even indirect language in a job posting can trigger an EEOC complaint if it appears to steer qualified candidates away because of a protected characteristic.

Common mistakes: Reusing old job ad templates without checking for outdated, non-compliant phrasing.

Summary

EEOC compliance isn't just an HR policy — it applies directly to how recruiters write job ads, screen candidates, and conduct calls. The safest approach is to keep every question tied to job-related skills and qualifications, and steer clear of anything about age, religion, family status, national origin, or disability.

This page is educational, not legal advice. When in doubt, consult your compliance team or an employment attorney.
