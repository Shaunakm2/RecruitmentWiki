Email & Telephone Etiquette

How to sound professional in writing and on the phone, especially when English isn't your first language.

Simple explanation: A good recruiting email has a specific subject line (role plus location), a short opening line stating why you're reaching out, two or three sentences on the role, and a clear call to action, like "Are you open to a quick call this week?"

Why it matters: Recruiters send dozens of emails a day, and candidates receive dozens from different recruiters — a clear, specific email stands out and gets a faster reply.

Common mistakes: Vague subject lines like "Exciting Opportunity!" that get ignored or marked as spam, and long emails that bury the actual job details.

Simple explanation: An effective voicemail states your name, your company, the reason for calling, and a callback number — spoken slowly enough that the candidate can write it down, usually under 30 seconds.

Why it matters: Most candidates won't call back a voicemail they can't fully understand or that doesn't explain why the call matters to them.

Common mistakes: Rushing through the callback number so fast the candidate has to replay the message multiple times just to catch it.

Simple explanation: On calls, speak at a moderate pace, pause to let the candidate respond, and reflect back what they said, e.g. "So it sounds like flexibility on start date matters most to you," to show you're actually listening.

Why it matters: Candidates can tell within the first minute whether a recruiter is genuinely listening or just reading a script, and it affects how much they trust and open up to you.

Common mistakes: Talking over the candidate or moving straight to the next question without acknowledging what they just said.
